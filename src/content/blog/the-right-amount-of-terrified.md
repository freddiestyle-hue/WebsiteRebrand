AI agents are a political spectrum now.

**Far right:** OpenClaw, Hermes, 14 unsupervised agents running at 2am, the founder's tab count as the only governance.

**Far left:** "I will never let an AI agent touch my customers, my CRM, my billing, or my reputation."

Both loud. Both confidently wrong in the way political extremes always are. The middle is the sane place. Live there.

## What is the right wing actually saying?

That something has changed. They're correct about that.

A founder in 2026 who refuses to use AI is competing with one whose Claude is drafting the first version of every email, the first read on every ticket, the first audit on every prospect. The gap is real. It widens weekly.

The right wing has noticed. They are sometimes annoying about it. They are also, often, not running businesses with customers, payroll, or legal exposure. The blast radius of their best agent screwing up is their evening.

If you only listen to the right wing, you will eventually let an AI send the email that costs you a customer. The right wing considers this acceptable collateral. You probably don't.

## What is the left wing actually saying?

That an unsupervised system inside a real business can do real damage. Also correct.

One wrong refund. One wrong customer email. One wrong CRM update. The cleanup takes a week and the founder is explaining to the team that yes, the AI did that, and yes, it was technically authorised because nobody specifically said it couldn't.

The left wing has watched what unsupervised humans do to systems and correctly extrapolated. Their mistake is conflating "unsupervised" with "AI at all." Plenty of AI usage is supervised, queued, approved, audited. They've thrown the whole category out because the loud version is reckless.

If you only listen to the left wing, you stand still while operators around you compound their AI advantage. The left wing thinks standing still is safe. It isn't.

## The middle position

The agent should not be autonomous. It should prepare every action.

That is the whole centrist platform.

The agent reads the inbound lead, classifies it, drafts the response, surfaces it. The human sends it.

The agent reads the support ticket, suggests the refund or workaround, drafts the message. The human approves it.

The agent watches the funnel, spots a drop-off, drafts a remediation, ranks the options. The human picks.

This is not "less agentic." It is more leveraged. You get 80% of the productivity at 5% of the blast radius. The agent does the work. The human keeps the judgment. The audit trail is clean because every action that left the building was approved by a named human.

We've written about the same pattern in [you do not need more agents, you need a kill switch](/blog/you-do-not-need-more-agents-you-need-a-kill-switch) and [the AI executive assistant is a queue, not a chatbot](/blog/ai-executive-assistant-queue-not-chatbot). Different angles, one idea: the queue is the product. The agent is incidental.

The right wing will say this isn't really autonomous. Correct. They've confused autonomy with value. The value is the work that got done, not the absence of a human in the loop.

The left wing will say they still don't want AI near their customers. They've missed the trick. In a queue, the agent never touches the customer. The human always does. The risk has been removed.

## Why do most operators end up on the extremes?

Because the middle is boring to talk about.

"I let my agents run free" is a Twitter post. "I will never trust AI with my business" is a LinkedIn humblebrag. "I built a queue where the agent prepares actions and I approve them in batches" is operating work. It doesn't go viral. It doesn't draw an audience.

The extremes get the airtime. The middle gets the results.

This is true of most political positions, in fairness. Centrism is unfashionable for the same reason every time: it doesn't feel like a stance. It just feels like adults thinking carefully about a thing. Nobody throws a parade for that.

## What does centrism actually require?

A small amount of effort up front. Most operators have never tried the middle path because nobody is yelling at them to.

The right wing wants you to install OpenClaw and turn it loose. The left wing wants you to ignore the whole thing. Neither asks you to do the slightly harder thing, which is: pick one workflow you currently do manually, have an AI tool draft the next action every morning, review the drafts before lunch, send only the ones that look right.

That's the entire centrist program. You can ship it this week. You don't need a runtime, a course, or a Twitter following.

## What's your actual position?

If you're on the right, when did you last check what your agents did overnight? When did one of them last touch a customer or a system without you noticing? Is the comfort earned, or just lucky?

If you're on the left, when did you last let an AI prepare a single decision for you to approve? Not run autonomously. Just prepare. If the answer is never, you're not protecting your business. You're protecting your discomfort.

Most operators picked their camp tribally, before they thought about it carefully. The interesting move in 2026 is to defect to the middle.

It is, as ever, where normal people live.

## FAQ

**Isn't a queue just "human in the loop" with a fancier name?**
Yes. The fancier name matters because "human in the loop" got used to describe everything from "the human supervises an autonomous agent" to "the human approves a single sentence." Calling it a queue is more honest about the shape: a ranked list of prepared items waiting for human action, with context attached. It's the difference between a kitchen ticket and a vibe.

**What if I trust my agent because it's been "tested"?**
Testing tells you the agent does the right thing in the cases you tested. It doesn't tell you what it does in the case you didn't think of. The queue model means you don't have to predict every edge case in advance. The human catches the case the test didn't.

**Doesn't this slow everything down?**
Less than you think. Approving prepared work is roughly 5x faster than authoring it from scratch. If your agent prepared 50 things overnight, you can approve all 50 before lunch. The throughput goes up, not down.

**Where do I actually start?**
Pick one workflow that currently lives in your head or a manager's. Write down the steps. Identify which one is "preparing the next action" - most of them are. Hand that prep to an AI tool you already have. Review the output every morning. That's tier 1. The fancy agent runtimes are tier 2 and can wait until tier 1 is a habit.

**Isn't this just a CRM with extra steps?**
A CRM is a database. A queue is a daily list of prepared, ranked, context-attached actions waiting for approval. The CRM tells you "you have 47 leads." The queue tells you "here are the 6 worth opening today, here's what to send each, here's why now." Same information. Different verb. The verb is the value.
